## Reaction time

- **Simple reaction time:** one signal, one response. It mostly reflects how fast you notice something and move your finger.
- **Choice reaction time:** several signals, a different response for each. You must decide first, so it takes longer.
- **Hick's law (Hick, 1952; Hyman, 1953):** the more options you have, the longer the decision takes. Time grows with each doubling of the number of options, not with each extra option.
- **Total time has parts:** noticing the signal, deciding, and moving. Only some of it is "thinking".
- **Donders (1868)** was among the first to split reaction time into stages by comparing simple and choice versions of a task.
- **Practice matters:** early tries are often slower because people are still working out the task. Times usually improve, then level off.
- **One score is a snapshot.** Tiredness, distraction, the device, screen delay and the input method all change it.
- **Plain-English line for pages:** "Your result shows how quickly you responded on this device, on this try. It's not a measure of how smart you are."

## Photographic memory and visual memory

- **Visual memory is real; "photographic" is mostly a myth.** Most people can hold a small number of items in mind for a few seconds, and that varies from person to person and from day to day.
- **Working memory (Baddeley & Hitch, 1974)** is the mind's short-term workspace. One part handles pictures and positions (the "visuospatial sketchpad"), one handles sounds and words.
- **Eidetic imagery** (very vivid, long-lasting mental pictures) is reported mostly in some young children and is rare and debated in adults. Claims of perfect "photographic" recall in adults have not been reliably shown.
- **Chunking:** grouping things into meaningful patterns makes them easier to hold. People who seem to have amazing memories usually use a strategy and a lot of practice.
- **Plain-English line for pages:** "This isn't a test of 'photographic memory'. It shows how many positions you could hold in mind right now."

## Number memory (digit span)

- **Short-term memory** is holding something for a few seconds. **Working memory** is holding it and doing something with it.
- **Forward span** (repeat the digits in order) is mainly holding. **Backward span** (repeat in reverse) also asks you to work with them. Keep this difference for the Learn article only; the test itself is forward.
- **Miller (1956)** popularised the idea that short-term memory holds only a handful of items, and that chunking helps. Later work suggests the limit depends on what the items are.
- **Ericsson & Chase (1982)** trained a volunteer to remember very long digit strings by linking digits to meaningful patterns. It shows that practice and strategy can stretch span.
- **Why people lose digits:** distraction, rushing, and trying to hold each digit separately instead of in groups.
- **Plain-English line for pages:** "Try grouping the digits (like a phone number). Most people find it easier."

## Attention span and sustained attention

- **Attention drifts for everyone.** Mind-wandering during a boring, repetitive task is normal.
- **Sustained attention (vigilance)** is the ability to keep watching for something rare over time. Performance often dips the longer a task runs.
- **Go/No-Go style tasks:** when you respond most of the time, holding back on the rare "don't" trial gets harder. Mistakes on those trials show how hard it is to stop an automatic habit, not that someone has "poor focus".
- **Steadiness:** how much your speed varies from try to try. It can change with sleep, stress and time of day.
- **Plain-English line for pages:** "This is a short task on one day. It's not a measure of your attention in general, and it's not a test for any condition."

## Stroop effect

- **Stroop (1935):** naming the colour of the ink is slower when the word spells a different colour (the word "BLUE" printed in red).
- **Why:** reading is so practised that it happens automatically, and it competes with naming the colour.
- **What it shows:** the extra time is a sign of your mind handling two competing responses. Everyone shows it.
- **What affects it:** practice, being tired, and the colour set. People with colour-vision differences may find it harder or easier for reasons unrelated to attention (the content doc has the colour-vision note).
- **Goal keeping (Kane & Engle, 2003):** when most trials are easy, people sometimes slip on the rare hard one because they stop actively keeping the goal in mind.
- **Plain-English line for pages:** "The slowdown on mismatched words is normal. It shows how strongly reading is wired in."
