You're tired. The lights are off. And suddenly your mind decides it's the perfect time to replay a conversation from three years ago.

## The 2 a.m. loop

Psychologists have a name for going over the same worries again and again without getting anywhere: *rumination*. It feels like problem-solving, but it usually isn't. You circle the problem instead of moving through it.

Most people do this sometimes. It becomes an issue when the loop keeps you awake, or when it leaves you feeling worse every time it runs.

> **Key idea** Thinking about a problem moves you towards a next step. Overthinking keeps you in the same spot, just more tired.

## What it looks like

It might be replaying a conversation and rewording what you said. It might be running through tomorrow's meeting for the tenth time, or building a chain of "what ifs" that ends somewhere worse each round. The thoughts feel urgent, but they seldom produce a decision.

## Why night makes it worse

**Fewer distractions.** During the day, work, people and your phone take up space. At night the unfinished worries finally get the room to themselves.

**A tired mind sees things bigger.** When you're worn out, problems can feel heavier and harder to put down than they do the next morning.

**Unfinished business.** Tasks you haven't closed, like a reply you didn't send or a plan you didn't make, tend to come back when nothing else is going on.

Most 2 a.m. problems are still there at 9 a.m. They just look smaller.

## Three things that help

**Empty your head onto paper.** Before bed, write down what's on your mind and tomorrow's to-do list. In one small sleep study, people who wrote a to-do list fell asleep faster than people who wrote about what they'd already done.

**Give worry a time slot.** Pick fifteen minutes earlier in the evening to think through what's bothering you. If a worry turns up at night, tell yourself: "That's for tomorrow's slot." The idea comes from older research on treating worry, and it works by teaching your mind that worry has a time and a place.

**Don't fight it in bed.** If you've been awake for a while, get up and do something calm and boring in dim light, then go back when you feel sleepy. Lying there wrestling with your thoughts tends to link the bed with being awake.

> **Side note** Rumination is different from reflection. Reflection asks "what can I learn?" Rumination asks "why me?" on repeat.

## A ten-minute evening routine

Try this for a week and see whether anything shifts.

1. **Earlier in the evening,** write down what's worrying you. Next to each item, add one small next step and when you could take it. If there is nothing to do, write that too.
2. **Before bed,** write tomorrow's to-do list, then close the notebook.
3. **In bed,** keep the lights low and put your phone away. Scrolling feeds your mind new things to chew on.

## What tends not to help

Arguing with your thoughts usually gives them more energy. Checking the clock makes the night feel longer. And trying hard to fall asleep tends to keep you more alert. If you notice yourself doing any of these, you aren't failing. It is just a cue to try something different.

## When it's more than that

If overthinking happens most nights, stops you from sleeping, or comes with feelings that are hard to manage, it's worth talking to a doctor or a mental-health professional. That isn't overreacting. It's looking after yourself.
