You finish a short quiz and it hands you a label or a number. For a moment it can feel as if the test saw something true about you. Sometimes it did. Often it caught how you felt for the last five minutes and not much else.

That gap is worth understanding, because the same few ideas explain almost every online psychology test, ours included.

## What a test actually does

A psychology test asks you to answer questions or do a small task under set conditions, then turns what you did into a number or a category. Two words come up whenever people judge how good a test is.

*Reliability* is about consistency: would a test give similar results under comparable conditions? *Validity* is about whether the evidence supports what someone wants to conclude from a score. A bathroom scale that repeatedly reads two kilos light illustrates the difference: a consistent reading can still be off target.

> **Key idea** A result describes how you did on this test, on this day. It does not describe all of you.

## Why one result is a snapshot

Our [reaction time test](/reaction-time-test/) records responses during a browser session. A questionnaire records the answers you chose. The result summarizes those entries; it does not explain why you made them.

If you repeat a test, you could compare the individual responses as well as the total. Keep a note of the task and setup so you know what you are comparing.

## Why we don't rank you

We do not rank your score against other people. That is deliberate. To compare a score fairly, someone has to collect results from a large, carefully chosen group doing exactly the same test in exactly the same way, and then check that the comparison holds up.

KnowPsyche doesn't collect your answers or your scores. They stay in your browser. So there is no group to compare you with, and any ranking we showed would be made up.

What we give you instead are bands: our own plain-language guide to reading a result. They are not compared with a population, and they are not a diagnosis. The [method page](/method/) explains how we build them.

## When a description feels right

Forer's work on personal validation concerns the gap between feeling that a description fits and showing that a test measures something accurately.

When a type description feels spot on, try asking: “What specific example makes this fit me?” Then look for an exception. The [Enneagram](/learn/what-is-the-enneagram/) is a good place to try this.

## What questionnaires miss

Some tests ask how you see yourself. Others watch what you do. Each has blind spots.

Our questionnaires summarize self-ratings. A task like the [Stroop test](/stroop-test/) records responses to a particular challenge. To think about work or relationships, bring in examples from those situations rather than relying on the score alone.

> **Side note** In 2015 a large team of researchers tried to repeat a hundred published psychology studies. Many of the results did not come out the same the second time. It is a fair reason to treat any single finding, and any single score, with some modesty.

## How to use a result well

- Treat it as a question. "Does this sound like me?" gets you further than "Is this me?"
- Check it against what you actually do. Keep what fits, and trust your own evidence where it doesn't.
- If a result surprises you, retake the test on another day before you read much into it.
- Look for patterns across several tests instead of leaning on one.
- Don't make big decisions about your job, studies or health on the back of a quiz.

## When to talk to someone

None of this is a clinical assessment. If memory, attention, mood or sleep is getting in the way of everyday life, talk to a doctor or a mental-health professional. A short online test can't tell you what is going on, and you deserve more than a guess.
